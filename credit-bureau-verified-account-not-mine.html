The Credit Bureau “Verified” an Account That Isn't Yours. Now What?

A “verified” response does not make incorrect information accurate. It means the bureau says its investigation confirmed the item. Your next step is a more specific, better-documented follow-up—not another vague dispute.

Collect the complete paper trail

  1. The credit report showing the disputed account.
  2. Your original dispute and every attachment.
  3. Proof of when the dispute was delivered or submitted.
  4. The bureau's investigation result.
  5. Records showing which names, addresses, dates, balances, or account details are wrong.

Identify the exact factual problem

“This account is not mine” states the problem, but a useful follow-up identifies the facts supporting your position. Explain which information is inaccurate or incomplete, why it is wrong, and what correction you are requesting. Send copies of supporting records rather than your originals.

If identity theft is involved

Use the federal recovery process at IdentityTheft.gov. Its recovery plan and identity-theft report can help you document fraudulent accounts and follow the correct blocking and dispute procedures.

If the second dispute still fails

Keep the complete record. Depending on the facts, your next step may include adding a statement to your credit file, disputing directly with the company that supplied the information, submitting a CFPB complaint after the dispute is no longer pending, or speaking with a consumer attorney.

Sources: CFPB dispute guidance and IdentityTheft.gov sample letter.
